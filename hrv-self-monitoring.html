Long-Term Self-Monitoring & HRV Discovery

I wore my self-built heart monitor over several weeks during normal daily life—working, coding, exercising, and resting. I discovered why one-size-fits-all health numbers fail: your personal baseline changes by up to 25 beats per minute depending on sleep and time of day. An algorithm must learn your personal baseline to be accurate.

01 · Overview

The Problem: 3-Minute Lab Tests Miss Real Life

Most scientific studies only track people for 3 minutes in a quiet lab. But a 3-minute snapshot completely misses how your body changes from morning to night as you tire out or recover.

Using my custom test rig, I recorded my own heartbeats for weeks during real daily life: deep programming blocks, reading, stressful moments, workouts, and evening rest.

02 · The Core Question

What was I trying to understand?

Can everyday heart rate variability accurately tell the difference between mental focus, physical exhaustion, and emotions—or do natural daily changes overwhelm the signal?

04 · Methodology

HRV Feature Extraction & Analysis

STAGE 01
Artifact Filtering
Applied point-process RR filter to eliminate premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) and motion spikes before calculating statistical intervals.
STAGE 02
Time-Domain Indices
Calculated rolling RMSSD (parasympathetic vagal tone marker) and SDNN (total autonomic power) across 3-minute sliding windows.
STAGE 03
Spectral LF/HF Ratio
Lomb-Scargle periodogram estimated Low Frequency (0.04–0.15 Hz) and High Frequency (0.15–0.40 Hz) bands to evaluate sympathovagal balance.
STAGE 04
Cognitive Task Segmentation
Segmented physiological recordings around difficult mental tasks: Sternberg holding, complex code debugging, and task switching.
05 · Empirical Results & Proof

Heart Rate Zones Across Discrete Emotional States

Cross-analyzing physiological recordings revealed distinct autonomic clusters. High-arousal states (Happy ~100 bpm, Anger & Surprise ~90 bpm) demonstrated marked sympathetic elevation compared to low-arousal states (Sad ~70 bpm, Fear ~70 bpm), providing biological boundaries despite subjective label noise.

CLUSTER 03

Personal resting offset varied by up to 25 bpm between morning rest and late-night exhaustion, confirming that universal fixed cutoffs fail.
06 · Forensic Post-Mortem

What Failed: The Collapse of Fixed Thresholds

The primary algorithmic failure was attempting to use static, absolute HRV thresholds (e.g., "RMSSD < 20 ms = High Stress").

The Baseline Shift Problem: On a well-rested morning, my resting heart rate was 62 bpm with an RMSSD of 48 ms. Following a poor night of sleep, my baseline resting heart rate was 78 bpm with an RMSSD of 22 ms while completely calm. A static classifier interpreted my calm state as acute emotional distress simply because the autonomic floor had drifted.
07 · Key Takeaway

What I Learned: Relative Deviation Over Absolute Values

Physiological interpretation is fundamentally relative. A drop of 10 ms in RMSSD is meaningful only when compared against the individual's rolling 7-day circadian baseline. Furthermore, mental task execution consistently produced transient cardiac deceleration upon cognitive intake—pointing toward evoked cardiac dynamics during working memory.

08 · Limitations

What This Investigation Does NOT Prove

  • N=1 single-subject tracking cannot establish population-level statistical generalizability on its own.
  • Confounders like caffeine consumption and unmeasured ambient temperature introduce uncontrolled autonomic variance.
  • Requires rigorous multi-participant psychophysiology validation in a formal laboratory experiment.
